A Safe Path to Producer-Direct Interstate Meat Commerce

A comprehensive 50-state policy, food-safety, market-structure, and intelligence analysis.

Final Distribution Edition v2.1Research cutoff: September 9, 2026Independent public-source analysis

Ranchers already have lawful ways to sell producer-owned inspected meat across state lines. The harder policy question is how to expand processing access without weakening public-health protections or damaging the large-scale infrastructure that downstream meat manufacturing and export markets depend on.

FIVE KEY JUDGMENTS

The debate often starts from the wrong premise.

These judgments summarize the evidence base. They are not presented as USDA or FSIS positions.

High confidence

Ranchers already have lawful interstate sales pathways.

Ranchers may own, brand, market, and sell producer-owned inspected meat across state lines when slaughter and processing occur through an authorized interstate inspection pathway. The policy problem is therefore the processing pathway—not whether producer-owned meat may be sold at all.

High confidence

The main barriers are legal and structural.

The custom-exempt “Not for Sale” structure, the interstate limit on ordinary State-inspected product, and the absence of State MPI/CIS access in Federal-only States define the principal constraints.

High confidence

Large packers are both a concentration concern and necessary infrastructure.

Large plants provide high-throughput slaughter, standardized further-processing inputs, byproduct markets, national distribution functions, and export production that smaller plants do not presently replace at national scale.

Moderate-high confidence

Legal permission alone will not create processing capacity.

Capital, labor, throughput, refrigeration, wastewater and rendering, inspection access, management capability, buyers, and committed customers determine whether regional capacity is durable.

Moderate-high confidence

Diversification is stronger than replacement.

A durable national reform should expand inspected producer-controlled capacity while preserving the public-health outcome and the large-scale industrial functions the broader meat system still requires.

WHAT THE CURRENT SYSTEM ALREADY ALLOWS

Several interstate pathways already exist.

The important distinction is not simply who owns the animal or brand. It is the inspection and processing status of the establishment preparing product for commerce.

INTERSTATE COMMERCIAL PATHWAY

Federal inspection

Federally inspected and passed meat may move in interstate commerce, subject to applicable labeling, records, handling, sanitation, HACCP, and other requirements. Producer ownership of the animal or finished brand does not itself prevent interstate sale.

SELECTED STATE ESTABLISHMENTS

Cooperative Interstate Shipment (CIS)

CIS permits selected small and very small State-inspected establishments in participating State MPI programs to ship interstate under a same-as-Federal framework with Federal oversight.

FEDERAL STATUS, STATE PERSONNEL

Talmadge-Aiken

Talmadge-Aiken agreements allow qualified State personnel to perform Federal inspection functions in federally inspected establishments. Product remains federally inspected and interstate eligible.

OWNER-USE, NOT ORDINARY COMMERCE

Custom exemption

Ordinary custom-exempt product is tied to the owner/household-use structure and must be identified “Not for Sale.” It is not an ordinary commercial interstate-sales pathway.

WHAT FEDERAL LAW ACTUALLY BLOCKS

The choke points are specific.

The administration's objective encounters existing statutory architecture rather than one blanket prohibition on producer-owned meat sales.

Custom exemption

21 U.S.C. § 623 structures the custom exemption around livestock and products used by the owner, members of the owner's household, nonpaying guests, and employees. Implementing regulations in 9 C.F.R. § 303.1 reinforce that owner-use structure and “Not for Sale” status. Broad commercialization of ordinary custom-exempt meat therefore requires more than an administrative reinterpretation.

Ordinary State inspection

21 U.S.C. § 661 establishes State meat-inspection programs for product distributed within the State. 21 U.S.C. § 683 separately creates CIS as the Federal statutory bridge that allows selected State-inspected establishments to ship interstate. That distinction is why ordinary State MPI status does not itself create broad interstate authority.

September 4, 2026 Executive Order

The Executive Order directs USDA to expand legitimate market opportunities, modernize inspection, strengthen State-Federal participation, identify statutory and regulatory barriers, and support processing capacity consistent with applicable law and food-safety protections. It does not itself rewrite the Federal Meat Inspection Act or convert ordinary custom-exempt meat into commercial interstate product.

WHY LARGE PACKERS STILL MATTER

Diversification does not mean pretending scale has no function.

A serious reform cannot assume that one slaughter plant is interchangeable with another. Large plants create continuous, standardized raw-material streams for retailers, foodservice, sausage manufacturers, patty makers, cooked-product facilities, and other further processors. They also recover value from hides, fats, organs, rendered materials, and other co-products and support export programs that depend on scale, segregation, certification, and country-specific eligibility.

Standardized inputs for further processors
Efficient co-product and byproduct markets
Large-volume export production

CUTLINE POSITION

More competitors. More redundancy. Preserve necessary system functions.

The objective should not be to replace large packers with small processors. It should be to build a more diversified processing system in which large, medium, small, cooperative, and producer-direct establishments perform the functions for which each is economically and operationally best suited.

THE PROPOSED PDIMC FRAMEWORK

Expand lawful inspected capacity without turning custom exemption into a commercial loophole.

PDIMC and Direct Producer Inspection (DPI) are Cutline-created policy recommendations. They are not existing FSIS programs, statutory authorities, or agency positions.

Expand CIS now.

Increase participation and usable capacity within the existing Cooperative Interstate Shipment architecture where State MPI infrastructure already exists.

Use Talmadge-Aiken selectively under strengthened oversight.

Use State personnel to support Federal inspection functions only with comprehensive training, clear supervisory accountability, enforcement alignment, regular coordination, dedicated oversight, and explicit start/maintain/terminate criteria.

Authorize a DIRECT-style interstate direct-to-consumer lane.

Congress could authorize limited direct-to-household interstate shipment from qualifying State-inspected systems while preserving State inspection, Federal program oversight, transaction records, recall capability, and clear product identification.

Create a federally authorized Direct Producer Inspection pathway.

DPI would be a new inspected commercial pathway for Federal-only States and qualifying producer-owned, cooperative, mobile, or regional models—not ordinary custom exemption under a different name.

Stage DPI product complexity.

Begin with lower-complexity raw intact products before moving into grinding, non-intact products, and more complex processing. This is a sequencing recommendation, not a claim that intact beef is free of microbial risk.

Build electronic lot traceability into DPI.

Require lot identity capable of linking producer, slaughter establishment, slaughter date, processing lot, and customer shipment as an additional safeguard for the proposed pathway.

Authorize a distinct DPI inspection legend or truthful identifier.

Product identification should communicate the pathway accurately without implying ordinary Federal inspection status if Congress creates a legally distinct lane.

Finance viable regional capacity—not buildings alone.

Evaluate projects against throughput, workforce, wastewater/rendering, cold chain, management capability, committed buyers, and sustainable utilization.

Keep competition enforcement and system-function protection running in parallel.

Capacity expansion does not replace Packers and Stockyards Act enforcement or antitrust policy. Reform should also monitor further-processing inputs, byproduct markets, export-qualified production, and national cold-chain capacity.

WHY NEBRASKA MATTERS

A national solution has to work in Federal-only States.

Nebraska is a useful test case because it does not operate an active State meat-inspection program. That means CIS cannot simply be expanded there under the existing architecture. A national proposal that works only in State MPI jurisdictions is incomplete by design.

The Federal-only test forces the policy question into focus: if Congress wants a broadly available producer-direct interstate lane, the architecture must provide a lawful inspected route for States without State MPI/CIS infrastructure rather than assuming every State will build a separate inspection bureaucracy.

30States operated meat-inspection programs under cooperative agreements with FSIS in 2026. A nationally complete strategy must also account for the remaining Federal-only States.

FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS

Clear answers to the core policy questions.

Can ranchers already sell their own beef across State lines?

Yes, when the animals and products move through an authorized interstate pathway, such as Federal inspection or qualifying CIS. The ownership or brand can remain with the producer.

Does the September 4 Executive Order legalize commercial custom-exempt meat?

No. The order directs USDA implementation and analysis, but it does not itself rewrite the Federal Meat Inspection Act or eliminate the statutory “Not for Sale” custom structure.

Does Cutline recommend replacing Federal inspection with custom exemption?

No. The recommended framework preserves inspection and proposes a new federally authorized, inspected DPI pathway only if Congress chooses to create one.

Why not simply break up the largest packers?

Competition concerns are real, but large packers also perform scale-dependent functions. The paper recommends more competitors and redundancy without assuming those industrial functions can be removed without replacement.

Would small processing automatically lower beef prices?

No. Small and local processing often has higher unit costs. The stronger case for diversification is producer choice, resilience, regional competition, and market access.

What is DPI?

Direct Producer Inspection is a Cutline-created policy proposal for a new inspected commercial pathway. It is not an existing USDA or FSIS program.
